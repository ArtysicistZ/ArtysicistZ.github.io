Why KV cache reuse is harder for vision than for language

June 2026 · ~ 10 min read

If you've spent any time around modern LLM inference, you've internalized one of its most important optimizations: KV caching. Every new token's keys and values are computed once, stored, and reused for the rest of the sequence. The savings are enormous and the math is exact — the cache is not an approximation of what attention would have produced; it is what attention would have produced.

It's natural to look at video vision transformers and ask the obvious question. Consecutive frames share most of their content — VideoMAE famously showed 90–95% temporal token redundancy in natural video. The encoder is the expensive part. Why not cache K and V across frames the way we cache them across tokens?

I spent several weeks on this question for a sparse-depth pipeline, read most of the relevant literature, and ended up where the field has converged: it does not work the way you'd hope. The reason is structural, not engineering — and it's worth understanding precisely, because the cleanest way to see why a technique won't port is usually to look at the assumptions it depended on in the place it came from.

The assumption that does most of the work

LLM KV caching works because LLM attention is causal. Token i only attends to tokens 0 through i. Once token i's key and value are computed, no future token can change them — k_i and v_i were computed from token i's embedding, which was computed from token i's representation in the layer below, which depended only on tokens 0 through i. Causality propagates cleanly down the stack.

That's the whole reason the cache is exact rather than approximate. The information that produced k_i and v_i is, by construction, frozen forever. Each new token extends the sequence without disturbing what came before.

ViT encoders use global self-attention. Every patch token attends to every other patch token, in every layer. If even one patch in the input changes — because that part of the scene moved, or because the camera shifted slightly — the correct K and V vectors for all patches change, because every patch's layer-1 representation was computed by attending to the now-changed patch, and every layer-2 representation was computed by attending to every layer-1 representation, and so on down the line.

This isn't an artifact of how anyone implemented attention. It's the math. Global self-attention is, by definition, a function in which every output depends on every input. There is no causal partition to exploit.

So what does "vision KV cache reuse" actually mean?

The proposals in the literature don't claim exact caching. They claim that for patches whose input tokens are similar to the previous frame's input tokens, the K and V vectors are approximately similar to the previous frame's K and V vectors — close enough that you can substitute them and the downstream task accuracy doesn't collapse.

This is a perfectly reasonable engineering proposition, but it's a different proposition. We've moved from caching (memoize a deterministic function) to approximating (substitute one value for another and hope the error is small). The accuracy floor, the error bounds, and the worst case all become things you have to reason about.

The most thoughtful work in this space is Eventful Transformers (Dutson et al., ICCV 2023). It introduces three coordinated mechanisms: a token gate that compares each token against a stored reference and selects only the tokens whose change exceeds a threshold for recomputation, a token buffer that fills unchanged positions from storage after sparse processing, and sparse attention updates that compute the QK⊤ changes selectively from delta-based queries and keys. On ImageNet VID detection and EPIC-Kitchens action recognition, it gets 2–4× speedup with minor accuracy loss, training-free.

That is a real, useful, deployable result. It's also worth noting what it doesn't do. It doesn't skip the patch projection — you have to compute the initial token embeddings to run the gate at all. The savings come from skipping self-attention for unchanged tokens, not from skipping the encoder. And the accuracy degrades under high motion, which is precisely where you most need it not to.

What the field has actually settled on

Look at where the state of the art has converged in video depth estimation — one of the most demanding domains for both efficiency and accuracy:

  • Video Depth Anything (CVPR 2025): recompute the encoder per frame, add temporal attention layers in the DPT head.
  • FlashDepth (ICCV 2025): recompute the encoder per frame, add a Mamba recurrent module (~1% of parameters) for temporal coherence.
  • StableDPT, Online Video Depth Anything: same pattern — encoder recomputed, temporal logic in the decoder.

There is essentially no recent video-depth method that caches the encoder. The one partial exception, VeloDepth, runs the full encoder on keyframes and uses optical-flow-warped features for intermediate frames — but it explicitly degrades under large motion and requires periodic full recomputation.

This is the field's empirical answer to the original question: encoder recomputation is cheap enough, relative to the cost of the errors that feature reuse introduces. Whatever savings you get from skipping the encoder are eaten by the temporal-module complexity you need to compensate for stale features.

What does port from LLM caching

Not everything from LLM KV-cache literature is dead on arrival in vision. Several techniques transfer cleanly because they operate on the cache's storage or shape, not on whether the values are still correct:

  • Grouped Query Attention. Share K and V heads across query heads. Works in vision the same way it works in language — the K/V representation gets smaller, attention output is mathematically identical up to the sharing. 4–8× KV memory reduction, requires retraining.
  • KV quantization. Store cached features in FP8 or INT4. Reduces memory by 2–4× with negligible accuracy cost. Training-free.
  • Sliding window attention. Bound the temporal context. Analogous to a fixed-window historical buffer in vision — the math is the same, you're just deciding how far back to remember.
  • Token eviction. Drop tokens that contribute little to subsequent computation. Evict3R shows this works for vision: 50% peak memory reduction, training-free.

These are all techniques that don't require the cached values to remain correct. They either reshape the cache, compress it, bound it, or prune it — without claiming that yesterday's K and V are still the right answer.

Where the deeper insight lives

I think the wider lesson here generalizes beyond attention. Whenever you port a technique across architectures, the first question to ask is: what assumption does this technique depend on? KV caching in LLMs depends on causality. Compiler memoization depends on referential transparency. Database materialized views depend on bounded update rates. Build-system incremental compilation depends on dependency declarations being honest.

When the assumption holds, the technique is exact and the savings are nearly free. When the assumption is approximately true, the technique becomes an approximation, and you've taken on a new responsibility: bounding the error. When the assumption doesn't hold at all, the technique either has to be redesigned around a different assumption (the recurrent-state and merge-and-compress lineages for vision are exactly this) or abandoned in favor of plain recomputation.

The reason "just cache it" tends to fail in vision is not that vision is harder than language. It's that vision attention was designed under a different set of priors, and one of those priors — any patch's representation can depend on any other patch's representation — is the thing the caching assumption needed to be false. The interesting engineering question stops being "how do we make vision caching work" and becomes "what's the cheapest way to express the assumption that does hold in video, namely temporal coherence in the output, not in the intermediate features." That question has produced the recurrent-state, token-merge, and sparse-recompute architectures that now win benchmarks.

Sometimes the right answer to "can we just borrow this trick" is no, and the more interesting work happens after you accept that.
