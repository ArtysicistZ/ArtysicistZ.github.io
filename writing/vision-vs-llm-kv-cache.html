Why KV cache reuse is harder for vision than for language

June 2026 · ~ 10 min read

Anyone who has worked on LLM inference knows the standard optimization: KV caching. Each new token's keys and values are computed once, stored, and reused for the rest of the sequence. The savings are large. The math is exact. The cache is not an approximation of what attention would have produced. It is what attention would have produced.

The same trick should work for video vision transformers, in theory. Consecutive frames share most of their content. VideoMAE measured 90 to 95 percent temporal token redundancy in natural video. The encoder is the expensive part of a video pipeline. There is enormous room for reuse. So why not cache K and V across frames the way they get cached across tokens?

I spent several weeks on this question for a sparse-depth pipeline, read most of the relevant literature, and ended up where the field has converged. It does not work in the way the analogy suggests. The reason is structural, not engineering. Looking at it carefully is useful: the cleanest way to see why a technique fails to port is to look at the assumption it depended on where it came from.

The assumption that does most of the work

LLM KV caching works because LLM attention is causal. Token i only attends to tokens 0 through i. Once token i's key and value are computed, no future token can change them. The information that produced k_i and v_i is, by construction, frozen forever. Each new token extends the sequence without disturbing what came before. Causality propagates cleanly down the stack. The cache is exact because the values it stores are exact.

ViT encoders use global self-attention. Every patch token attends to every other patch token, in every layer. If even one patch in the input changes, because that part of the scene moved or because the camera shifted slightly, the correct K and V vectors for all patches change. Every patch's layer-1 representation was computed by attending to the now-changed patch. Every layer-2 representation was computed by attending to every layer-1 representation. Down the line.

This is not an implementation artifact. It is the math. Global self-attention is, by definition, a function where every output depends on every input. There is no causal partition to exploit.

What “vision KV cache reuse” actually means

The proposals in the literature do not claim exact caching. They claim that for patches whose input tokens are similar to the previous frame's input tokens, the K and V vectors are approximately similar to the previous frame's K and V vectors. Close enough to substitute without collapsing downstream accuracy.

That is a reasonable engineering proposition. It is also a different proposition. The technique has moved from caching (memoize a deterministic function) to approximating (substitute one value for another and hope the error is small). Accuracy floors, error bounds, and worst cases become things that have to be analyzed.

The most thoughtful work in this space is Eventful Transformers (Dutson et al., ICCV 2023). It introduces three coordinated mechanisms. A token gate compares each token against a stored reference and selects only the tokens whose change exceeds a threshold for recomputation. A token buffer fills unchanged positions from storage after sparse processing. Sparse attention updates compute QK⊤ changes selectively from delta-based queries and keys. On ImageNet VID detection and EPIC-Kitchens action recognition, the result is 2–4× speedup with minor accuracy loss, training-free.

That is a real, useful, deployable result. The savings come from skipping the expensive attention layers for static tokens, not from skipping the encoder. The patch projection still runs. The gate evaluation still runs. And the accuracy degrades under high motion, precisely where the speedup matters most.

What the field has actually settled on

Look at where the state of the art has converged in video depth estimation, one of the most demanding domains for both efficiency and accuracy. Video Depth Anything (CVPR 2025) recomputes the encoder per frame and adds temporal attention layers in the DPT head. FlashDepth (ICCV 2025) recomputes the encoder per frame and adds a Mamba recurrent module (around 1% of parameters) for temporal coherence. StableDPT and Online Video Depth Anything follow the same pattern. Encoder recomputed. Temporal logic in the decoder.

No recent video-depth method caches the encoder. The one partial exception, VeloDepth, runs the full encoder on keyframes and uses optical-flow-warped features for intermediate frames. It explicitly degrades under large motion and requires periodic full recomputation.

This is the field's empirical answer to the original question. Encoder recomputation is cheap enough relative to the cost of the errors that feature reuse introduces. Whatever savings come from skipping the encoder are eaten by the temporal-module complexity needed to compensate for stale features.

What does port from LLM caching

Not everything from the LLM KV-cache literature is dead on arrival in vision. Several techniques transfer cleanly because they operate on the cache's storage or shape, not on whether the values are still correct.

Grouped Query Attention. Share K and V heads across query heads. Works in vision the same way as in language: the K/V representation gets smaller, attention output is mathematically identical up to the sharing. 4 to 8× KV memory reduction. Requires retraining.

KV quantization. Store cached features in FP8 or INT4. 2 to 4× memory reduction with negligible accuracy cost. Training-free.

Sliding window attention. Bound the temporal context. Analogous to a fixed-window historical buffer in vision. Same math, just a choice of how far back to remember.

Token eviction. Drop tokens that contribute little to subsequent computation. Evict3R demonstrates this for vision: 50 percent peak memory reduction, training-free.

These share a property. None of them require the cached values to remain correct. They reshape the cache, compress it, bound it, or prune it. They do not claim that yesterday's K and V are still the right answer.

Where the deeper insight lives

The wider lesson generalizes beyond attention. Whenever a technique gets ported across architectures, the first question is: what assumption does this technique depend on? KV caching in LLMs depends on causality. Compiler memoization depends on referential transparency. Database materialized views depend on bounded update rates. Build-system incremental compilation depends on dependency declarations being honest.

When the assumption holds, the technique is exact and the savings are nearly free. When the assumption is approximately true, the technique becomes an approximation, and a new responsibility appears: bounding the error. When the assumption fails entirely, the technique either has to be redesigned around a different assumption or abandoned for plain recomputation.

“Just cache it” fails in vision not because vision is harder than language. It fails because vision attention was designed under a different set of priors, and one of those priors, that any patch's representation can depend on any other patch's representation, is exactly the thing the caching assumption needed to be false. The interesting engineering question stops being how to make vision caching work. It becomes what is the cheapest way to express the assumption that does hold in video: temporal coherence in the output, not in the intermediate features. That question has produced the recurrent-state, token-merge, and sparse-recompute architectures that now win benchmarks.
