On knowing what changed in a vision pipeline

June 2026 · ~ 8 min read

Suppose you're processing a video, one frame at a time, through a vision transformer. Most of the scene between frame t and frame t+1 hasn't changed. The intuitive optimization is right there: don't recompute the unchanged parts. Skip the static patches, only spend FLOPs on what actually moved, and pay for what you use.

This is one of those ideas that looks obvious until you sit down to implement it. Then a quiet circularity emerges, the kind that takes a while to articulate and longer to accept. To skip the unchanged tokens, you need a way to identify them. Every way of identifying them is either too coarse to be safe or too expensive to be worth it.

The first approach you'll try is pixel-level change detection. The second approach you'll try is feature-level change detection. The third approach you'll try is a learned change predictor. Each fails for a different reason, and the failures rhyme.

Pixels are noisier than you'd hope

It is tempting to think of "the scene hasn't changed" as a property of the pixels: if the L2 distance between corresponding patches in consecutive frames is small, the patch is unchanged. Try this for a few minutes on real footage and you'll discover what every video codec engineer already knows: nothing in a video is truly static.

Camera sensors have shot noise. Auto-exposure and auto-white-balance shift global statistics frame to frame. A handheld camera jitters at the sub-pixel level. Outdoor lighting changes continuously. Even with a tripod, an indoor scene under fluorescents flickers at frequencies the sensor partially aliases. The "L2 distance between patches" histogram for two consecutive frames of a static scene is not concentrated at zero — it's spread across a wide band, and your threshold will either accept too many false positives (treating moving regions as static, contaminating downstream features) or reject too many true negatives (recomputing nearly everything, defeating the optimization).

Codec people solved a version of this problem decades ago with motion estimation, sub-pixel alignment, and rate-distortion optimization — H.264 and H.265 are essentially industrial-grade change-detection systems. The cost is high. A modern video encoder is not free, and even with hardware acceleration it has nontrivial latency. If you're spending that much compute to identify which patches changed, you've already done a sizable fraction of the work you were trying to avoid.

Features change in ways pixels don't

The natural fallback is to compare at the feature level. Maybe the pixels of a moving shadow change, but the high-level features stay roughly stable, and what matters for downstream tasks is the feature representation. So compare layer outputs frame to frame, gate on feature similarity, and recompute when features have actually drifted.

This logic is sound. The implementation reveals the trap: to know whether the features changed, you have to compute the features. The whole point was not to compute the features. You've replaced one full encoder pass with one full encoder pass plus a comparison step, which is strictly worse.

Real systems get around this by comparing features at an earlier layer (cheaper) and gating later-layer recomputation (the expensive part). Eventful Transformers (Dutson et al., ICCV 2023) does exactly this: it computes the initial patch embeddings always, then uses thresholded gating to decide which tokens get their self-attention layers recomputed. The savings are real (2–4× on detection and action recognition benchmarks) but they come from skipping the expensive attention layers for static tokens, not from skipping the encoder entirely. You still pay the patch projection, and you still pay the gate evaluation.

And the gate has its own failure mode. The decision rule is local — "is this token's input similar to its previous input?" — but the consequence is global, because once you skip a token's recomputation, the cached K and V for that token become approximations whose error propagates into every other token's attention output. Local gating, global error. The accuracy degrades under high motion, and it degrades in a way that is hard to bound a priori.

The learned-predictor approach, and why it usually doesn't pay

You can train a separate, lightweight network to predict which tokens will change. Now you don't depend on pixel statistics or running the encoder partially — you have a dedicated model that takes the previous frame's features and the current frame's pixels and outputs a change mask.

This works in principle. In practice, the predictor has to be accurate enough that its mistakes don't degrade downstream accuracy. That accuracy bar implies the predictor needs nontrivial capacity. Once you've spent that capacity, the predictor's cost competes with the encoder cost it was supposed to save. The good predictors are not cheap, and the cheap predictors are not good. You end up doing useful work to decide which work to skip, and the savings collapse.

The pragmatic version of this approach uses scene priors instead of learned predictors. MaskVD (2024) combines static masks (regions that are usually background in driving scenes) with dynamic masks (previous-frame detections plus a small motion buffer) to mask out 80% of input patches while maintaining detection performance. This works because the prior is genuinely task-specific: in driving, you mostly care about the road, and the road behaves a particular way. The technique does not generalize to depth estimation, or to indoor agents, or to any domain where the relevant content can appear anywhere in the frame. You are paying for accuracy with assumptions about your data distribution, and the bill comes due when your data shifts.

The shape of the problem

This circularity — to skip work, you need information that requires work to produce — isn't unique to vision. It's a recurring pattern wherever someone tries to make a computation incremental:

  • LLM speculative decoding. Use a small draft model to predict the next few tokens, then verify with the big model. The cheap predictor is acceptable because the verifier catches errors cheaply (one forward pass over a few tokens). The math works because verification is parallelizable; if it weren't, the whole scheme would collapse.
  • Build systems. Incremental builds only work when dependency graphs are honestly declared. The moment you have an undeclared dependency (an environment variable, a clock-dependent test, an implicit include), incremental compilation either silently corrupts or has to fall back to a clean build — doing more work, not less.
  • Database materialized views. View maintenance is incremental only when the view's defining query supports differential evaluation. For general queries, view refresh is just a recomputation.
  • Cache coherence. CPU caches work because the hardware tracks every write at line granularity, paying real silicon area and power for the bookkeeping. The reason this is hidden from us is that the cost was paid in the architecture; we don't see it because someone else did.

In each of these, the same trade-off appears. To avoid redoing work, you need to track what changed. The tracking has a cost. The optimization only wins when the tracking cost is small relative to the work it lets you skip. When the tracking cost approaches the work itself — as it does for ViT encoder features, where "what changed" is a question whose answer requires the encoder — the incremental approach loses to plain recomputation.

The cheapest way to know

The thing I find quietly funny about all of this is that the field's most successful response has been to stop asking the question. Video Depth Anything, FlashDepth, StableDPT, Online Video Depth Anything — none of them try to identify which tokens changed. They recompute the encoder on every frame and put the temporal logic in the decoder, where it's both cheaper to run and easier to reason about. The encoder is treated as a stateless image-feature extractor; the temporal coherence comes from a small Mamba block or a few cross-attention layers downstream. Recomputation, it turns out, is cheap enough.

This is not a defeat for the original intuition — "don't redo work you've already done" is still a good intuition. It's a correction about where the cost lives. The encoder is expensive but stateless, so recomputation has bounded cost and bounded latency. The temporal logic is cheap but stateful, so caching there is both safe (the state is small and bounded) and useful (it captures the cross-frame structure you actually care about).

When the question "what changed" is itself expensive, the cheapest move is often to stop asking, recompute the cheap-to-recompute parts, and put your caching effort where the math allows the cache to be small. The incremental approach is not always wrong, but it has prerequisites — cheap change detection, bounded error propagation, or an architecture that respects locality — and when those prerequisites aren't met, holding onto the optimization just because it sounds good costs you both accuracy and engineering time.
