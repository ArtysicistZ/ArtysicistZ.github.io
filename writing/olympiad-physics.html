On Olympiad physics and engineering intuition

June 2026 · ~ 6 min read

The problem about the magnet

One of the first problems I remember from olympiad training was a magnet falling through a copper tube. Given: the magnet's mass, the tube's conductivity, the geometry. Asked: the terminal velocity. The point of the problem is not to plug numbers into a formula. The formula is the easy part. The point is to see what is happening. Induced eddy currents resist motion. They dissipate energy as heat. The magnet falls slower than it would in free air. Once that picture is clear, the equation almost writes itself.

I think about that problem a lot when debugging systems.

Two ways of knowing

There are two ways of doing physics. The first I will call formula-first: memorize the equations, recognize which one applies, solve. It works for textbook problems and breaks immediately when the problem is not textbook. Olympiad training is the opposite. The first instruction is not to reach for the equation. The first instruction is to ask what the system wants to do. What is resisting it. Where the energy goes.

That habit transferred almost cleanly when I started writing code.

Three places it shows up

  • Latency budgets. When profiling a hot path, the first question is not “which line is slow.” It is “where would the time have to go.” Cache miss? Branch prediction? Allocation? The habit is to estimate before measuring, the same way physicists estimate before computing.
  • Distributed systems. A consumer is lagging behind a producer. The textbook answer is “backpressure.” The physics answer is closer: there is a flow rate, there is a downstream resistance, the queue is the capacitor that absorbs the difference. Once the system reads as a circuit, the design choices clarify.
  • Training dynamics. When a loss curve flattens earlier than expected, the question is not “is the learning rate wrong.” It is “what gradient signal is the model not receiving.” The diagnosis has the same shape as “why is the magnet falling at terminal velocity already.”

Where the analogy breaks

I do not want to oversell this. Physics intuition fails me in at least three places.

  • State machines. Conservation laws do not help. Each case has to be tracked.
  • Distributed consensus. The failure modes are combinatorial, not continuous.
  • Anything involving humans. Still working on this one.

Why I keep going back

What olympiad physics gave me was not a technique. It was a habit: look at the system before solving. That habit has been more useful than any single algorithm class or framework. It is also the part of how I think that I most want to protect as the years pass. The easy default, after a few years of writing code, is to recognize patterns and skip the look.

I try not to skip the look.
